**Important population interpretation:** An older IUCN 2012 estimate (published on its 2020 page) gave roughly **6,600 adults** and **1,409 mature individuals**. The 2025 assessment estimates **1,676 mature individuals**, and explicitly explains that newly discovered populations and changed estimation methods account for the higher mature-individual total. **Do not infer the species has recovered**, or compare 6,600 adults with 1,676 mature individuals: they describe different categories and time periods. The IUCN estimates an approximately **16.9% decline across three generations** using comparable observations. [Source][iucn]

## How to recognise one

African wild dogs have very large rounded ears, long slender legs, a dark muzzle and a variable patchwork of tan, russet, black and white. The patch pattern differs among individuals, so it can support *re-identification when carefully confirmed*; photographs alone do not establish an animal's name. The broad pale tail tip and white-patched lower legs are visible in Josh's images. Taronga describes **four toes on each front paw**, without the dewclaw characteristic of domestic dogs. [Taronga, 2023][taronga-day]

They are a distinct wild canid lineage. Their common names do not mean they are hybrids of dogs or wolves. The name *pictus* refers to their painted or patterned appearance.

## Ecology and behaviour

### Social lives and pups

These are strongly social pack animals. They hunt cooperatively and share food and duties caring for pups. At Dubbo, Taronga's accounts from its historic breeding program describe older siblings transporting food and helping watch younger pups. Packs ordinarily concentrate most breeding in a dominant breeding pair, although the IUCN assessment recognises occasional subordinate breeding. Large litters are possible; a Taronga account reports litters as large as **15–18 pups**. [Taronga breeding archive][litter] · [IUCN][iucn]

### Moving, hunting and communicating

They are adapted for sustained movement across large territories. Taronga reports speeds **up to 70 km/h** and describes yips, low contact calls and collective rally calls. Taronga also cites a **80–90% hunting success** figure, but this should be understood as a zoo educational generalisation, **not** a universal success rate for every population, season or hunting method. [Taronga, 2023][taronga-day]

**Photo-specific caution:** two dogs walking near one another in an enclosure is evidence of proximity and locomotion. It is **not enough** to infer pack hierarchy, hunting, stress, reproductive state, age or sex.

### Range and habitats

Native to sub-Saharan Africa, with remaining populations mainly in southern and eastern Africa, plus much smaller areas in central and western Africa. IUCN lists use of savanna, woodland/forest, shrubland, grassland and some arid habitats. Large connected landscapes are vital: the species uses extensive territories even where it is relatively common. The IUCN reports the largest surviving subpopulation in the **Kavango–Zambezi (KAZA)** transfrontier region spanning southern African countries. [IUCN][iucn]

## Why endangered?

The IUCN's 2025 review identifies interacting causes:

1. **Habitat loss and fragmentation** diminish available land, prey access and links between packs.
2. **Conflict with people** includes deliberate persecution, lethal control and livestock-related conflict.
3. **Snares and road collisions** injure and kill free-ranging animals.
4. **Rabies and canine distemper**, including exposure through domestic dogs, can cause sudden crashes.
5. **Population isolation and demographic instability:** small and wide-ranging populations are harder to maintain; packs can fragment if key breeders die.

The IUCN notes that some apparent population increases since older assessments are from discovering previously unknown packs rather than verified recovery. [IUCN assessment][iucn]

## Taronga Western Plains Zoo, Dubbo

- The zoo has held African wild dogs **since 1982**, according to Taronga's archive. [Source][pups]
- Taronga recorded successful breeding and large litters historically, including a litter of **11 pups born on 25 August 2016** to Kimanda and Guban. [Source][litter]
- On **30 August 2023**, Taronga named **four females** in its care: **Mambo, Spot, Bayana and Masego**. **This is a dated 2023 snapshot, not verification of current membership or the individuals in these photos.** [Source][taronga-day]
- On **2 February 2024**, Taronga announced a **$1.25 million African wild dog exhibit redevelopment**, including a replacement moat wall and planned enhanced visitor viewing. This is a **published plan**, not independent confirmation each stage has since finished. [Source][development]
- Current Taronga *African Adventure* itinerary lists the habitat at **19B**; always check official visitor information for access and times. [Source][itinerary]

**Unknown as of this research:** names, exact age, sex, parentage, number of dogs currently housed, and whether the 2024 building works are fully completed. These should come from a keeper or a newer dated official zoo update, not inferred from archive articles.

## Conservation work worth supporting

Taronga states it has supported **Painted Dog Conservation** in Zimbabwe's Hwange area, particularly work protecting animals from snares and treating injured animals. Painted Dog Conservation describes anti-poaching patrols, veterinary rehabilitation, pack monitoring, school education and community projects intended to improve long-term coexistence. [Taronga][pups] · [Painted Dog Conservation][pdc]

**Practical actions:** learn and share accurately sourced conservation information, support accountable field projects, and respect animal welfare and zoo visitor rules. Do not assume a zoo animal is being prepared for reintroduction unless the zoo specifically confirms this.

## Interesting questions for a keeper

1. Which individuals were in the enclosure on the date of this visit, and how can their markings be identified reliably?
2. How are food-sharing and group feeding behaviours managed here?
3. Have enclosure/viewing renovations been completed, and which features support natural behaviours?
4. Which regional zoo breeding or genetic-management program does the pack currently participate in?
5. How does Taronga currently support wild painted dog conservation on the ground?
