# Black rhinoceros — Dubbo Zoo researched guide

**Species:** *Diceros bicornis* · **IUCN category:** Critically Endangered (CR) · **Reviewed:** 18 October 2026. **Type:** zoo research, not a personal photo sighting.

## Identification and way of life
The African black rhinoceros has a pointed, prehensile **upper lip** used to grasp leaves and twigs. Despite their name, black and white rhinos are both generally greyish. Black rhinos are chiefly **browsers**, taking shrubs and woody plants; they are often more solitary and less grazing-oriented than white rhinos. Both African species usually bear **two keratin horns** (not bone). A horn is not a defence against modern high-powered poaching weapons.

## Where they belong
Black rhinoceroses survive in fragmented populations within eastern and southern Africa. Poaching for the illegal rhino-horn trade, habitat changes, limited genetic connectivity and the needs of small isolated populations are leading conservation problems. Effective protection includes trained rangers, intelligence and prosecutions, community participation and secure viable range areas; captive breeding alone does not secure wild habitat.

## Numbers: year and unit matter
The [International Rhino Foundation's September 2025 report](https://rhinos.org/blog/news-room/sotr2025/) estimates **6,788 wild black rhinos**, up from the **2022 estimate of 6,195**. Both are estimates across all ages, **not** IUCN mature-individual counts and **not** the number in Dubbo. A gain in the species estimate does **not** remove the Critically Endangered status; local groups face different risks.

## Taronga Western Plains Zoo record
Taronga describes Dubbo as having the **most successful black-rhino breeding program outside Africa**, and offers an ordinary public viewing location at **map 16C** on its [African Adventure itinerary](https://www.taronga.org.au/dubbo-zoo/plan/get-the-most-out-of-your-visit/african-adventure). Some animals also live in a separate *behind-the-scenes* conservation breeding facility. Taronga's [Black Rhino Experience](https://www.taronga.org.au/dubbo-zoo/encounters-and-experiences/animal-encounters/black-rhino) explains the distinction; booking and schedules can change.

In a [2 February 2024 historical announcement](https://www.taronga.org.au/about/newsroom/media-releases/black-rhino-calfs-first-mud-bath-at-dubbo-zoo), Taronga reported female calf **Sabi Star** and mother **Bakhita**, with the calf approximately one month old. This is **not** evidence that either one was photographed, publicly on exhibit or remains at Dubbo in October 2026. The 2025 IRF figures are global wild estimates, not zoo inventory.

## Careful visitor field notes
Look for the **hook-shaped browsing lip**, two horns, neck/shoulder shape, and browsing versus grazing. Keep a safe distance and follow keeper guidance. Mud bathing may help thermoregulation and skin care; mud alone is not evidence of illness.

## Clarification: species risk and wild trend are different

[IUCN's March 2020 African rhinoceros update](https://iucn.org/news/species/202003/conservation-efforts-bring-cautious-hope-african-rhinos-iucn-red-list) says **black rhinoceros remained Critically Endangered (CR)** but numbers had **slowly increased** through monitored recovery years due to conservation efforts, despite significant historic declines. The report quotes roughly **4,845 wild animals in 2012 and 5,630 in 2018**. CR is based on extinction-risk criteria/history, so *critically endangered* does not imply a decreasing *recent* count in every population. Use the 2025 International Rhino Foundation surveys for a newer annual headcount and keep their estimation method separate.
