# Cheetah — Dubbo Zoo deep research guide

**Scientific name:** *Acinonyx jubatus* · **IUCN:** Vulnerable (VU) · **Reviewed:** 18 October 2026. **Evidence:** Taronga's dated Dubbo reports and published zoo itinerary. No uploaded personal cheetah sighting.

## Biology and adaptations
Cheetahs have a lightweight runner's frame, long legs, semi-retractile claws that help traction and a long tail used for balance while cornering. Their dark **tear marks**, from the inner eye toward the mouth, and solid spots help distinguish them from spotted leopards. Cheetahs rely on acceleration and agility during relatively short chases rather than indefinitely maintaining their top speed. An advertised maximum sprint speed is **not** normal travelling speed.

Female cheetahs are generally solitary except when raising cubs, whereas males may form coalitions, frequently with brothers. Hunting depends on prey, cover and opportunity. Cheetahs can purr and chirp, but unlike lions are not typically roaring big cats.

## Conservation and how to interpret numbers
Habitat loss and fragmentation, depletion of prey, conflict around livestock, illegal wildlife trade and small/isolated populations all threaten the species. Taronga's [May 2025 report](https://www.taronga.org.au/about/newsroom/posts/cheetah-delivery-at-dubbo-zoo) states **fewer than 7,000 mature wild cheetahs**, but that is a *mature individual* estimate, not all wild cheetahs of every age; it must not be compared directly to older estimates of all animals.

A managed regional breeding programme can maintain a genetically diverse backup population and advance veterinary knowledge, but zoo birth counts are not evidence that wild range and prey have recovered.

## What Taronga actually reported
Dubbo is a participant in the international cheetah breeding programme; Taronga says it was the first Australasian zoo to breed the species. The [African Adventure itinerary](https://www.taronga.org.au/dubbo-zoo/plan/get-the-most-out-of-your-visit/african-adventure) marks cheetahs at **9G**, with the specific planned 12:15 viewing stop potentially subject to change.

On **22 February 2025**, female **Siri** delivered via emergency Caesarean after early labour. Taronga's **14 May 2025** update describes four cubs: two stillborn, one later died of pneumonia, and one surviving female receiving intensive veterinary support. Siri recovered, but the survivor could not be reunited with her; the article described ongoing hospital care at the date of publication. **Do not assume the juvenile is publicly visible or has any specific 2026 health status.** This is a historical medical report that deserves accurate, non-sensational treatment.

Siri was herself a keeper-supported cub, as described in [Taronga's 2018 life-history article](https://www.taronga.org.au/about/newsroom/posts/siri-the-cheetah-cub-continues-to-thrive). That context can explain assisted rearing but does **not** imply all cheetah mothers reject single cubs.
