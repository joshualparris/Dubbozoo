# Beyond the basics: seven newer elephant research findings (2024–2026)

**Research reviewed:** 15 November 2026 (Australia/Sydney)  
**Primary relevance:** Asian elephant (*Elephas maximus*) at Taronga Western Plains Zoo; African elephant findings are explicitly labelled.  
**Evidence:** published primary research, not observations or diagnoses of Dubbo's individual elephants.

## 1. Trunk whiskers are tactile instruments, not just hairs — February 2026

**Direct research:** Schulz and colleagues, *Science*, 12 February 2026, [doi:10.1126/science.adx8981](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adx8981); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41678607/).

Researchers examined whiskers covering **Asian elephant** trunks, studying their shapes, internal architecture and mechanical response. They reported a thick, circular, porous, comparatively stiff base grading into a thinner, more oval, denser and softer tip. Modelling and mechanical analysis indicate that the amplitude and frequency of vibration transmitted to the whisker base can vary with *where* contact occurs along a whisker. This provides a plausible mechanism for precise touch despite a robust, thick-skinned trunk.

**Why it matters:** The trunk may detect small surface features through its whiskers while manipulating food and objects; a visitor can watch the trunk make contact with logs or vegetation without assuming a specific thought, intention, or mood. The research concerns material mechanics and sensory encoding, not a validated measure of any pictured elephant's sensitivity or experience.

## 2. The trunk's upper and lower skin do different mechanical jobs — June/July 2026

**Direct research:** Lo Preti and colleagues, *PNAS Nexus*, published 16 June 2026, [doi:10.1093/pnasnexus/pgag164](https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgag164); [published figure correction, 30 July 2026](https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgag260); [source data](https://doi.org/10.17632/w8jhjwwkzm.1).

A mechanical and microscopy study of Asian elephant trunk skin found the **dorsal (outer/upper curvature)** side comparatively rough and protective, whereas the **ventral (inner/lower curvature)** side was more compliant for wrapping/grasping objects. In the reported initial tensile-modulus comparison, dorsal skin was **3.14 times stiffer** (0.22 versus 0.07 MPa). The authors' finite-element model also suggests subsurface dermal papillae on the ventral side may concentrate forces near sensory structures.

**Important methodological limit:** The published experimental programme relied principally on material from **one adult Asian elephant** that died at Zurich Zoo; the associated dataset also mentions access to an adult and juvenile trunk. Measurements of sampled tissues and computational predictions should **not** be represented as a demonstrated universal ratio for every elephant or as a live measurement of touch perception. Figure 2 was corrected after publication; use the corrected article.

**Why it matters:** Together with finding 1, this offers a testable biological explanation of how a single trunk can tolerate rough contact and still grasp delicate items. These are separate studies of whiskers and skin, not evidence that either animal in Josh's photos was performing a particular task.

## 3. Asian elephants have shown nuanced *tool use* with water hoses — 2024

**Direct research:** Urban and colleagues, *Current Biology*, online 8 November 2024, print 2 December 2024, [doi:10.1016/j.cub.2024.10.017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2024.10.017); [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39520985/).

In Berlin Zoo observations, a female Asian elephant named **Mary** used a water hose to bathe herself, changing where she gripped the hose depending on whether she wanted the flow close to her body or over her back. Another elephant, **Anchali**, repeatedly kinked and compressed a hose, reducing water flow. The paper describes distinct handling strategies and individual differences; in controls with multiple hoses, the researchers could **not establish that Anchali specifically targeted Mary's hose**.

**What not to claim:** The work does not establish that Asian elephants generally use hoses in the wild, that Anchali was intentionally 'jealous' or 'sabotaging' another animal, or that any similar behaviour occurred at Dubbo. The interesting finding is flexible manipulation of a physically complicated object.

## 4. AI can identify elephant sounds, but a model does not automatically travel well — September 2025

**Direct research:** Avicena and colleagues, *Biological Conservation* 309, article 111272, [doi:10.1016/j.biocon.2025.111272](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biocon.2025.111272).

Using recorded **Asian elephant** calls and non-elephant sounds, the best convolutional neural-network classifier achieved **98.45% average test accuracy** with balanced samples in the reported validation design. But when trained on Sri Lankan elephant recordings and tested on previously unseen Malaysian elephant recordings, performance fell to **67.93% average accuracy**. Some acoustical features differed between the geographic groups.

**Why it matters:** Passive acoustic listening might eventually help warn nearby communities about wild elephants, but local training data, realistic validation and human response plans are vital. These numbers are *dataset test results*, not guarantees of real-world village-warning accuracy. The paper explicitly raises research ethics, protection of endangered species and rights of local communities; publishing exact locations of vulnerable animals can cause harm.

## 5. Camera alerts plus trained responders are a promising coexistence approach — November 2025

**Direct research:** Rastogi and colleagues, *Conservation Science and Practice*, published 11 November 2025, [doi:10.1111/csp2.70186](https://doi.org/10.1111/csp2.70186).

A field deployment in **West Bengal, India**, combined automated camera-based elephant detection with alerts and rapid response. The paper reports **266 elephant detection events**, average alert transmission of approximately **42 seconds**, and average field-team arrival of **18.2 minutes**. Its abstract reports no human fatalities or serious injuries in the deployment period, compared with documented casualties in earlier years in the study area.

**Causal caution:** It is an applied field study, **not proof that the AI alone prevented all incidents**. Teams, geography, changing elephant movements and other interventions matter. The system is a conservation-management example for wild Asian elephants, not a proposed public tracking system for Taronga's captive elephants.

## 6. Giving elephants choice in daily care may improve aspects of welfare — published 11 November 2026

**Direct research:** Doornbusch and colleagues, *Frontiers in Ethology*, [doi:10.3389/fetho.2026.1995029](https://doi.org/10.3389/fetho.2026.1995029).

An **Australian** study followed **six Asian elephants at Melbourne Zoo**, comparing a relatively fixed husbandry routine with a later more flexible routine. The revised approach offered more variation in food/access, less fragmentation of the herd and more time for the elephants to choose activities. The paper, *published in November 2026*, analyses **historical observations from 2018–2020**, not a present-day 2026 zoo census.

Across the two analysed conditions, the researchers reported the following *proportions of observed behaviour*:

| Behaviour category | Earlier routine | Revised routine | Interpretation |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| Abnormal repetitive behaviour | 10.55% | 6.26% | Lower after changes |
| Anticipatory behaviour | 14.17% | 8.16% | Lower after changes |
| Feeding/foraging/drinking | 29.97% | 39.34% | Higher after changes |
| Wallowing and other self-maintenance | 2.59% | 6.35% | Higher after changes |
| Social behaviour | 3.57% | 1.36% | Also lower: mixed outcome |

**Reading it correctly:** The design is a small, non-randomised, before/after observation of one herd; other environment, staff and individual-health changes also occurred. The reductions in repetitive behaviour are promising, but **not a universal causal estimate**. Lower social and environmental-engagement proportions matter, too. Crucially, **the study is about Melbourne Zoo, not an audit of Dubbo's keeper practices**, and a pair of still photographs cannot assess an elephant's welfare.

**Good keeper question:** What choices do Dubbo's elephants have about shade, substrate, browsing, social contact and participation in voluntary training?

## 7. 'Name-like' calls have been demonstrated in *African* elephants — important species boundary

**Direct research:** Pardo and colleagues, *Nature Ecology & Evolution*, 10 June 2024, [doi:10.1038/s41559-024-02420-w](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41559-024-02420-w); note the journal's 2025 author correction.

Machine-learning analysis of **wild African savanna elephants** found individually addressed calls, and playback experiments found different responses when an elephant heard a call originally addressed to it versus a call for another elephant. The evidence suggests some calls refer to specific recipients without simply copying each recipient's own voice.

**Not transferable by assumption:** This is **not direct evidence that Asian elephants use the same individual-name system**. The species, populations and methods must be preserved when explaining it to children. It does give us a worthwhile comparative research question about social communication across the elephant family.

## Field-study prompts for the next visit

1. Watch what the **tip and underside of the trunk** contact. Can you describe the object and movement without assigning the animal a motivation?
2. Record a short sequence of behaviour if the zoo allows it, then distinguish **observation** (trunk curled) from **interpretation** (possibly smelling/handling something).
3. Ask a keeper which **choices** elephants have about habitats and training; do not infer this from fence design or a snapshot.
4. Ask which noises can be heard by visitors versus the **low-frequency vocalisations** used by elephants; don't claim an unheard rumble occurred.
5. Ask what Taronga does for **wild elephant coexistence** and how its programmes evaluate actual outcomes, not only awareness.

- **Original information already present:** broad anatomy, social groups, IUCN status, basic threats, Taronga transfers/herd history, and the earlier five-photo set — see [existing guide](asian-elephant.md).
- **New in this document:** six Asian-elephant studies (whisker mechanics, skin mechanics, tool use, cross-region bioacoustics, camera-response coexistence, Australian husbandry), one **African-only** comparative communication result, and two new photo-copy records.
- **Primary research checked:** the linked study abstracts and, where accessible, original published article text. This is an AI-assisted synthesis, **not** new animal research, a veterinary assessment or an independent field replication.
- **No live Taronga claim:** No individual photographed elephant is named; no conclusion about the zoo's actual November 2026 animal roster or welfare is made.
- **Publication-date distinction:** The Melbourne Zoo study was published 11 November 2026, although its observed routines date to 2018–2020. The skin paper has a July 2026 correction; the African calls paper has a 2025 author correction.
