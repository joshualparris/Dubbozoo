## 2. Species or species complex? The taxonomy is unsettled
Galápagos tortoises occur naturally **only** in the Galápagos archipelago, Ecuador. Island and volcano populations diverged, producing recognisable **domed** and **saddle-backed** shell shapes. The shapes can relate to forage height, vegetation structure and local histories, **but they are not a DNA test and cannot assign a zoo individual to an island**.

There are legitimately different taxonomic conventions:
- The [Galápagos Conservancy biodiversity page](https://www.galapagos.org/about_galapagos/biodiversity/) refers to island lineages as **separate species** in the genus `Chelonoidis`, counting 15 named forms across historical/modern lineages. Other organisations describe 12 living lineages.
- The [2025 Turtle Taxonomy Working Group checklist](https://iucn-tftsg.org/wp-content/uploads/crm.10.checklist.atlas_.v10.2025.pdf) proposes a more conservative treatment of these island populations as **subspecies of one species, *Chelonoidis niger***, pending further genomic work.
- Some legacy zoo pages simply use **Galápagos giant tortoise** as a broad group, with a single **Vulnerable** or **Endangered** label, without identifying the precise lineage.

**This catalogue deliberately uses `Chelonoidis sp. (Galápagos giant tortoise complex)` and leaves `iucn_status` null.** The conservation status of a specific island population must be derived from a taxon-matched, versioned assessment, not from shell curvature or the status of another island's tortoises.

## 3. Biology — extraordinary slow-living herbivores
A giant tortoise is a **reptile**, not a mammal; it has a bony **carapace** connected to the spine and ribs and a lower **plastron**, not a shell it can step out of. These tortoises are primarily herbivorous, eating grasses, leaves, fruit, cactus pads and other plants. They can help shape island plant communities as **large grazers and long-distance seed dispersers**. Adults can reach hundreds of kilograms and live **well beyond a century**, though individual ages require keeper records. Dubbo's **2023** adult group, for example, was described as 130–270 kg and roughly 50–100 years old — a dated group estimate, not this animal's measured weight or age.

Strong legs support their very heavy bodies. The head and front legs in these photos may look rough or wrinkled, a normal anatomical appearance, not evidence of disease. **Don't touch, feed or cross barriers**: even normally slow-moving giant tortoises can have powerful bites or crush risks, and specialist diets support their wellbeing.

Sources: [Galápagos Conservancy ecosystem account](https://www.galapagos.org/projects/rewilding-galapagos/rewilding-giant-tortoises/), [Galápagos Conservation Trust fact sheet](https://galapagosconservation.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Galapagos-giant-tortoise-facts.pdf), [Taronga's 2023 records](https://www.taronga.org.au/about/newsroom/posts/where-are-the-tortoises).

## 5. Threats and genuine conservation success
Historic exploitation by sailors and introduced species devastated island populations. **Rats and pigs** eat eggs and hatchlings, **goats** alter vegetation and compete for food, and fire ants and other invasive organisms can endanger young tortoises. Development and climate changes add risks. Survival varies greatly by island; some lineages remain at high risk even when other islands recover.

Conservation is more than a zoo breeding programme: monitoring, invasive-species eradication, recovering protected habitat, rearing juveniles and **releasing them to the right islands** can help rebuild ecological function. The [Galápagos Conservancy's rewilding account](https://www.galapagos.org/projects/rewilding-galapagos/rewilding-giant-tortoises/) reports **over 9,000** (with programme counters updated over time) captive-reared/released tortoises, an island ecosystem benefit rather than a claim about Dubbo zoo releases.

A concrete example: [Pinzón rat eradication](https://www.galapagos.org/conservation/project-pinzon/) in **2012** was followed by the return of naturally hatched young in **2014**, after about a century without successful recruitment. A new [Floreana lineage restoration in February 2026](https://www.galapagos.org/newsroom/a-homecoming-for-floreana/) is promising, but returning mixed-ancestry tortoises is **not genetic resurrection** of an unchanged extinct population.

## 6. Family questions and research limits
- Is the shell more **domed** or **saddle-shaped**? Why might a tortoise need to reach different plants?
- Can you spot the **scutes** (large plates) and strong front legs?
- How might swallowing fruit help **move seeds across an island**?
- Why do a **50-year-old** tortoise and a **five-year-old** tortoise need different care?
- Why could protecting eggs from rats be even more important than protecting strong adults?
