## 2. Why giraffe taxonomy changed in 2025

The old convention grouped giraffes as *Giraffa camelopardalis* with several subspecies. After integrating genomic, skull/morphological and geographic evidence, the **IUCN SSC Giraffe and Okapi Specialist Group formally endorsed four giraffe species in August 2025**:

| Species (2025 IUCN taxonomy) | Scientific name | GCF 2025 wild estimate, all ages |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Northern giraffe | *Giraffa camelopardalis* | 7,037 |
| Reticulated giraffe | *Giraffa reticulata* | 20,901 |
| Masai giraffe | *Giraffa tippelskirchi* | 43,926 |
| Southern giraffe | *Giraffa giraffa* | 68,837 |
| **Combined approximate estimate** | | **140,701** |

These are **GCF's 2025 wild population estimates**, not a count of captive zoo giraffes, and **not IUCN 'mature individual' figures**. Sources: [IUCN reclassification, 21 Aug 2025](https://iucn.org/press-release/202508/four-giraffe-species-officially-recognised-major-conservation-reclassification), [GCF State of Giraffe 2025](https://giraffeconservation.org/state-of-giraffe/) and [full report](https://giraffeconservation.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/State-of-Giraffe-2025-Report-v5.pdf).

**Important conservation-status caveat:** Taronga's [general animal page](https://www.taronga.org.au/wildlife-and-conservation/animals/detail/giraffe) still shows the historical single-species name *Giraffa camelopardalis* and **Vulnerable**. That is the *legacy combined-giraffe assessment*, not a specific identification of the photographed animals. The 2018 IUCN assessment lists the former *reticulated subspecies* (*G. camelopardalis reticulata*) as **Endangered**, but this must **not** automatically be attached to Dubbo's unidentified animals. IUCN's 2025 taxonomy update will guide new species-level Red List assessments. [IUCN reticulated assessment (2018)](https://doi.org/10.2305/IUCN.UK.2018-2.RLTS.T88420717A88420720.en); [GCF conservation status explanation](https://giraffeconservation.org/giraffe-conservation-status/).

### What their markings can — and can't — tell us

GCF describes reticulated giraffes as having relatively geometric reddish patches separated by sharply defined pale lines. The giraffes in these photos look broadly similar in pattern, but zoo populations may have managed/complex ancestries and **appearance is insufficient for a taxonomic assignment**. Taronga's own published species information does not, in the sources reviewed, confirm the ancestry of these particular animals. Keep the photographic record at `Giraffa sp.` until zoo records provide a definitive classification.

GCF uses **individually unique coat patterns** for photo-based monitoring in Africa. Such patterns can assist with later re-identification **only if matched to reference images**; they do not give us an animal's name on their own. [GCF: why giraffes have spots](https://giraffeconservation.org/facts-about-giraffe/why-do-giraffe-have-spots/).

## 3. Biology — things to notice on a visit

- **Ossicones, not antlers:** the paired, skin-covered bony structures on their heads help distinguish giraffes from other animals; calves are born with ossicones lying flatter before they rise. The foreground animal's are visible. [Taronga: giraffe calf trio](https://www.taronga.org.au/about/newsroom/posts/giraffe-calf-trio-reaching-new-heights).
- **Seven neck vertebrae:** giraffes have the same number of cervical vertebrae as humans, just greatly elongated. [Taronga: African Savannah](https://www.taronga.org.au/dubbo-zoo/precincts-and-pitstops/african-savannah).
- **Browsing specialists:** their mobile tongues and lips help select leaves and shoots, including from thorny trees. The tongue is approximately 45–50 cm long, often dark blue/purple toward the tip; that colour's precise evolutionary explanation remains uncertain. [GCF tongue explainer](https://giraffeconservation.org/facts-about-giraffe/how-long-is-a-giraffes-tongue-what-color-is-it/).
- **Patterns aid identity:** unlike a zebra's stripes, their unique irregular patch patterns can be tracked in photos across time; GCF reports using photographic re-identification at large scale. [GCF spots explainer](https://giraffeconservation.org/facts-about-giraffe/why-do-giraffe-have-spots/).
- **Calves and mothers:** Taronga describes calves approximately 2 metres tall at birth and a long gestation. Taronga's general page gives 13–15 months, while its older calf announcement gives 14–16 months, so a useful family-friendly summary is **roughly 15 months**, not a falsely exact figure. [Taronga animal guide](https://www.taronga.org.au/wildlife-and-conservation/animals/detail/giraffe); [Taronga 2022 calf release](https://www.taronga.org.au/about/newsroom/media-releases/new-baby-giraffe-calf-twpz).
- **Giraffe food at Dubbo:** in a 2023 keeper article, Taronga described lucerne hay, measured pellets, carrots during encounters and harvested browse, including eucalypt and wattle; these are **historical husbandry details**, not necessarily today's menu. [Taronga 2023 keeper article](https://www.taronga.org.au/about/newsroom/posts/world-giraffe-day-2023).

## 5. Wild conservation — neither despair nor false recovery claims

GCF's **2025** report estimates **about 140,700 wild giraffes across the four species**. Its reported higher totals than older counts reflect a mix of actual conservation outcomes and **improved survey coverage**; a change in estimates cannot be interpreted as uniform recovery. Reticulated giraffes remain highly threatened: **20,901 estimated wild individuals** and cautiously encouraging local trends, with data gaps where field work is difficult. Habitat conversion and fragmentation, illegal hunting and human–wildlife pressures remain key threats. For conservation, browse primary sources rather than using a misleading single global status for all four species. [GCF State of Giraffe 2025](https://giraffeconservation.org/state-of-giraffe/); [GCF reticulated species page](https://giraffeconservation.org/giraffe-species/reticulated-giraffe/); [IUCN taxonomy review](https://iucn.org/resources/file/evaluation-taxonomic-status-giraffe-giraffa-spp).

Taronga participates in Australasian coordinated breeding and manages genetic diversity through transfers. This is valuable ex-situ conservation **but not equivalent to demonstrated improvements in wild populations**. [Taronga 2023](https://www.taronga.org.au/about/newsroom/posts/world-giraffe-day-2023); [Taronga 2025](https://www.taronga.org.au/about/newsroom/posts/keepers-raise-giraffe-calf-at-dubbo-zoo).
