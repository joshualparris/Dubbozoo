# Greater one-horned rhinoceros — Dubbo Zoo deep research guide

**Scientific name:** *Rhinoceros unicornis* · **IUCN:** Vulnerable (VU) · **Reviewed:** 18 October 2026. No personal photo observation recorded.

## Ecology and identification
Sometimes called the Indian rhinoceros, the **greater one-horned rhino** has a single keratin horn, dramatic skin folds and a broad body adapted to tropical floodplain grasslands, rivers and wetlands. Its thick folded skin does not make it armoured like metal; it needs healthy skin, water and mud. The lip grasps and processes grasses and vegetation, and the animal can eat aquatic plants and browse. These animals may spend substantial time in water to cool off, but are also strong terrestrial movers.

Most of the remaining population is concentrated in **India and Nepal**. Their recovery demonstrates that habitat protection, anti-poaching and community participation can work, but concentration in a few floodplain protected areas also creates vulnerability to flooding, disease, encroachment and connectivity barriers.

## Conservation with dated numbers
Taronga categorises the species as **Vulnerable**, despite improvements from very low historical numbers. The [International Rhino Foundation 2025 report](https://rhinos.org/blog/news-room/sotr2025/) estimated **4,075 wild greater one-horned rhinos**, versus **4,014** in the preceding reported global series. This is an approximate **all-ages wild animal** metric, not breeding individuals or zoo animals; avoid using it as a precise 2026 census.

Threats continue from poaching, habitat conversion, competition for resources and small isolated subpopulations. A successful reintroduction or increase does not justify ending protection.

## What is documented at Dubbo?
Taronga says these rhinos can be seen in its **Wild Asia habitat** and lists a **12:55 pm keeper talk on weekends and school holidays**. Its map and experiences change, so check close to a visit. Taronga also notes its partnership with the International Rhino Foundation on rhino conservation in Africa, India and Indonesia.

A recent Taronga Sydney article mentions **Hari**, who was **born at Dubbo and later relocated to Sydney**; his appearance on the Sydney Rhino Reserve page is specifically **not** evidence he still lives in Dubbo.

## Questions to ask at the enclosure
- How does one horn differ from the two-horned African species?
- Does the animal browse, graze or take aquatic vegetation?
- Where does it have opportunities to wallow, shade and withdraw?
- Why does a recovering species remain Vulnerable?
