## 2. Biology: it is a mongoose, not a prairie dog
Meerkats are **small carnivoran mammals**, not rodents, despite their upright sentinel posture. They are native to the **semi-arid landscapes of southern Africa** — especially South Africa, Botswana, Namibia and neighbouring regions. Their fur is beige to grey-brown with darker back barring, dark patches around the eyes (helpful against glare), narrow bodies, powerful front claws for **digging**, and a balancing tail with a darker tip. The animal in this photo is partly turned away, so several diagnostic details cannot be checked.

Adults commonly weigh about **0.6–1 kg** and are roughly **25–35 cm body length** plus a comparatively long tail, though figures vary among studies and locations. They are typically **diurnal**, using underground burrow systems to shelter from temperature extremes and predators.

Sources: [Smithsonian profile](https://nationalzoo.si.edu/animals/meerkat), [Animal Diversity Web species account](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Suricata_suricatta/).

## 3. Life in a mob: sentries and cooperative parenting
A **mob** can include a dominant breeding pair, their offspring and additional helpers. Other adults assist with pup care and foraging, while different individuals may take turns standing **sentry**. Sentries use characteristic alarm calls to distinguish danger contexts and signal responses; **not every upright meerkat is standing sentinel**. Groups may clash violently over territory; avoid the myth that they are uniformly peaceful or everyone mates equally.

Meerkats eat mainly **insects and other invertebrates**, as well as small vertebrates, eggs and some plant material. They search by digging and locating hidden prey. They can sometimes consume venomous scorpions; describing them as 'completely immune to all scorpion venom' oversimplifies complex prey handling and physiological resistance. In the wild, helpers can provision young and teach them how to handle prey.

Pregnancy lasts roughly **11 weeks**; litters often contain **two to five pups**, which develop in sheltered burrows. Helping behaviour and group size vary with resource availability and social conditions. [Smithsonian](https://nationalzoo.si.edu/animals/meerkat); [Animal Diversity Web](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Suricata_suricatta/); [Meerkat research, Science (2006)](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1128727).

## 4. Conservation status — Least Concern is not zero risk
The [Smithsonian zoo](https://nationalzoo.si.edu/animals/meerkat) and [Animal Diversity Web](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Suricata_suricatta/) list *Suricata suricatta* as **Least Concern**. This generally indicates the species is comparatively widespread and not currently approaching global IUCN threatened-species thresholds. It **does not** mean every local population is secure: drought, habitat change, disease and severe weather can influence survival at particular sites.

Don't invent a precise number of meerkats worldwide. The available sources do **not** provide a defensible global census.

## 5. Taronga Western Plains Zoo — two exhibit locations in its history
The zoo has documented **more than one meerkat habitat**:
- In [July 2018](https://www.taronga.org.au/about/newsroom/posts/new-meerkat-exhibit-now-open), Taronga announced a new meerkat exhibit near **African wild dogs and black rhinos** toward the beginning of the zoo circuit. It described large glass viewing panels, sand, climbing logs, burrows and heat panels.
- The later **Waterhole precinct** includes a separate meerkat habitat, visitor tunnel/pop-up window and café, listed at **map 3R** ([current precinct guide](https://www.taronga.org.au/dubbo-zoo/precincts-and-pitstops/waterhole)). The [2019–20 annual report](https://taronga.org.au/sites/default/files/2021-01/Taronga_Annual_Report_2019_2020.pdf) says Waterhole opened **23 June 2020** with an integrated meerkat habitat. In a [historical World Meerkat Day post](https://www.taronga.org.au/about/newsroom/posts/world-meerkat-day), Taronga described **five** animals in that Waterhole group and **two** visitor-visible meerkat groups overall at the time. The page shows a confusing reposted date (2024) while describing its **COVID closure in 2020**, so **do not apply those numbers to October 2026**.

The one supplied glass-front photo is consistent with *a* Dubbo meerkat exhibit but **does not prove which habitat**. In published visitor information, [Meerkat Encounter](https://www.taronga.org.au/dubbo-zoo/encounters-and-experiences/animal-encounters/meerkat) meets at **The Waterhole**, usually **9:50 am** with conditions/booking rules. That is **not** an invitation to place fingers into enclosures or feed the animals. Observe through the visitor facilities; supervise young children.

## 6. Five field observations to try
1. Count how many animals you can **actually see**, without assuming the mob size.
2. Is one looking up while another **digs**? Watch longer before calling it sentry duty.
3. Where are burrow openings, shade and warm spots?
4. Can you spot darker patches around the eyes and stripes on the back?
5. If you hear calls, can you describe them without guessing what each call means?
