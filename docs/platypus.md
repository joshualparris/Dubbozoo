# Platypus — Dubbo Zoo deep research guide

**Scientific name:** *Ornithorhynchus anatinus* (Shaw, 1799) · **Order:** Monotremata · **IUCN:** Near Threatened (NT; assessment cited by Taronga) · **Reviewed:** 18 October 2026.
**Record type:** verified species and zoo research, **not a claim that Josh photographed a platypus**.

## What makes the animal extraordinary
Platypuses are one of the living monotreme lineages: mammals that lay eggs instead of giving birth to live young. Female platypuses incubate generally 1–3 eggs in a breeding burrow and feed young with milk secreted onto skin rather than through nipples. Their relatives are echidnas. They have waterproof fur, powerful webbed forefeet, a flattened tail that stores fat, and eyes, ear openings and nostrils that close during dives.

The bill is a highly sensitive sensory organ, not a duck's hard beak. Electroreceptors detect electrical activity of moving prey, supplemented by touch-sensitive mechanoreceptors. Platypuses find aquatic insect larvae, crustaceans and other freshwater invertebrates on the bottom of rivers and creeks, carrying food in cheek pouches to chew at the surface. **Electroreception is not echolocation**; avoid repeating older educational material that conflates the two.

Males have venom-delivering ankle spurs, especially relevant during the breeding season. They are **not safe to handle**; severe pain can follow a spur injury, even though human deaths are not the normal outcome. Avoid all handling of wild platypuses.

The species occupies waterways of eastern mainland Australia and Tasmania, with patchy local occurrence. A river corridor may look intact yet be ecologically broken by weirs, bank clearing, sedimentation or impassable barriers. Platypuses are generally solitary and often most readily detected at low-light periods, but daytime activity occurs.

Sources: [Taronga species page](https://www.taronga.org.au/wildlife-and-conservation/animals/detail/platypus), [2019 scientific synthesis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6479513/).

## Conservation — use the right listing
Taronga's species profile lists **IUCN Near Threatened**. The 2019 synthesis and 2021 UNSW-led national review describe the IUCN 2016 Near Threatened assessment. Do not substitute an old Taronga construction announcement calling platypuses 'vulnerable' for a current, nationally applicable legal classification. Different jurisdictions and assessment systems have different criteria and review dates; a recommendation to list is **not a completed legal listing**.

Principal threats include altered flows and drought, severe floods and bushfires, fragmentation by dams and other barriers, riverbank degradation, sediment, extraction of water, invasive pressures and drowning in unsuitable enclosed fishing traps. Population trends are difficult to measure because animals are cryptic and historical baselines are scarce. **Do not invent a global population number.**

Sources: [UNSW national assessment (2021)](https://www.unsw.edu.au/content/dam/pdfs/science/ces/research-reports/2021-10-platypus/2021-10-platypus-1.pdf), [Taronga species profile](https://www.taronga.org.au/wildlife-and-conservation/animals/detail/platypus), [review in Mammalia](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6479513/).

## Platypus Rescue HQ — what is verified about Dubbo
On **15 February 2024**, Taronga officially opened Platypus Rescue HQ on Dundullimal Tubba-Gah Wiradjuri Country at Taronga Western Plains Zoo. The **$12.5 million** facility integrates research, wildlife rescue and rehabilitation, disaster refuge, specialised pre-release conditioning and a **public viewing habitat**. Its published *emergency refuge capacity* is **up to 65 platypuses** — **not** a count of residents at any moment. Taronga lists a **2,800 m² facility**, **50 × 7,000-litre tanks** and **25 earth-filled burrow tubs**; capacity and infrastructure should not be confused with confirmed occupancy.

Taronga identified the visitor habitat's adult male **Mackenzie ('Mack')** in 2024–2025 and continues to mention him on the precinct page. That supports a named historical/current published exhibit reference, **not identification in any user photo**. In February 2025 Taronga announced four additional platypuses in a controlled breeding-biology research programme supported by Taronga, UNSW and San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance. Some research or rescue platypuses are deliberately not public-facing. The centre is a conservation facility, not simply a display aquarium.

At the time of review, Taronga's visitor page specifies **map 21M, end of zoo circuit**, **9 am–4 pm** for the precinct and a **2:45 pm keeper talk on weekends and school holidays**. Times are subject to change. Check the official page before relying on them.

Sources: [Opening announcement, 15 Feb 2024](https://www.taronga.org.au/about/newsroom/posts/platypus-rescue-hq-opens), [Rescue HQ official visitor guide](https://www.taronga.org.au/dubbo-zoo/precincts-and-pitstops/platypus-rescue-hq), [2025 research partnership](https://www.taronga.org.au/about/newsroom/posts/taronga-collaborates-with-international-partners-to-conserve-the-iconic-platypus), [first anniversary, 19 Feb 2025](https://www.taronga.org.au/about/newsroom/posts/platypus-rescue-hq-turns-1).

## Ecology and welfare: interpreting a sighting
An animal surfacing for air and diving again is normal behaviour. A closed bill and a shallow pool alone do not establish hunger, distress or illness. Water quality, burrow access, feeding and veterinary routines in an aquarium need professional oversight; a visitor should not infer care quality from a snapshot.

**If seen in the wild:** observe quietly from the bank; never approach, feed, capture or relocate. If entangled, injured or visibly in danger, contact the relevant licensed wildlife rescuer rather than attempting to handle its spurs or bills.

## On your visit: five observation prompts
1. Did you actually see an animal? If yes, describe surfacing and direction of travel without inferring an individual name.
2. Can you spot how the broad front feet propel it and the tail follows as a rudder?
3. Does the information panel distinguish electroreception from hearing or echolocation?
4. How might an upstream barrier alter a platypus population even when a waterhole remains?
5. What is the difference between **65-place disaster capacity** and animals currently housed?

**Evidence limit:** no platypus photo, individual identity or date-specific sighting has been submitted to this journal. Add an observation only when supported by new primary material.
