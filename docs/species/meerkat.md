## Species identity and identification

Small slender mongoose with eye patches, pointed snout, long tail and a characteristic upright sentinel posture. This is a species of mongoose, not a prairie dog or a domestic cat.

**Native range / habitat:** Open dry regions of southern Africa including Kalahari ecosystems. Where the exhibit label lacks a confirmed scientific identification, this description is **conditional** on future species verification.

## Feeding, behaviour and natural history

Lives in cooperative groups with complex social relationships. Groups defend territories, use burrow systems and forage for invertebrates, eggs and small vertebrates. Some adults watch while others dig; sentinel roles rotate and should not be labelled as a lifelong individual job.

**Field question:** How does cooperative lookout behaviour support a group digging for food? Look for an observable feature or action rather than assuming an unseen purpose, personality, sex, health or family relationship from a single image.

## Wild conservation and scientific interpretation

Not a globally rare species, but exposed to local pressures from habitat disturbance and persecution. Captive social welfare depends on suitable group composition, digging substrate and retreat space.

**Status caution:** Least Concern (IUCN; taxon-matched recheck advised). Assessment authority, taxonomic rank, year and whether quoted quantities count all individuals or only mature breeders should be matched before adding headline population figures. Historical counts and today's population should never be substituted for one another.

## Specific Dubbo zoo evidence and history

Taronga's Waterhole precinct has a meerkat habitat beside family facilities; historic reports document a newly developed display. A quiet daytime posture is not enough to diagnose an individual animal's welfare.

## Zoo-field observation and evidence protocol

Observe from designated public areas, noting the actual date/time, site map grid, number visible and visible feeding/resting/moving/social interactions. Record habitat features, weather and vantage point when known. Do not diagnose health, captivity stress or breeding success from one still photo. Conservation breeding supports genetic management but does not replace protection of wild habitat or prove reintroduced survival.
