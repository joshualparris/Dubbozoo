## Scientific identification and adaptations

Burrowing marsupial with sandy/grey coat, broad blunt skull and hairy muzzle. It differs from the bare-nosed/common wombat and northern hairy-nosed wombat; photographs require facial detail and keeper ID.

## Food, reproduction and ecological roles

Grazes dry native grasses, using burrow systems to avoid heat and dryness, with slow metabolism and large hindgut fermentation. Female has backwards-facing pouch that reduces soil ingress during digging.

**Field question:** Which physical detail separates hairy-nosed and bare-nosed wombats, and why does that matter for conservation? Record time, location, number seen and directly observable actions without guessing health, social status or individual name.

## Threats, conservation and scientific limitations

Drought, sarcoptic mange, habitat/soil disturbance and local colony isolation. The northern hairy-nosed wombat is a different species and its extreme rarity must not be attributed to southern hairy-nosed wombats.

The assessment shown here is a dated conservation/legal context, **not a fresh population estimate**. Legal rankings in NSW, federal Australia and the IUCN may differ; wild, domestic, conservation-breeding and hospital animals must remain separate. For unknown taxonomy, no precise species-level Red List label should be assigned until confirmed.

## IUCN reassessment checked: Least Concern, published 2025

The [IUCN assessment published in 2025](https://doi.org/10.2305/IUCN.UK.2025-1.RLTS.T40555A258654451.en) (assessed **19 November 2024**) categorises *Lasiorhinus latifrons* **Least Concern (LC)**, replacing the **Near Threatened** category of 2016. This update takes into account strong populations in the Gawler Ranges and Western Australia despite declines in certain local populations. A global LC label does **not** mean all regions are secure or disease-free.
