# White rhinoceros — Dubbo Zoo researched guide

**Scientific name:** *Ceratotherium simum* · **Reviewed:** 18 October 2026 · **No personal sighting or photo verified**.

## Which rhinoceros?
The white rhinoceros is not white: the name's origin is debated, and grey skin cannot reliably identify a species. The clearest field distinction from the black rhino is the **broad, nearly square, grass-cropping mouth** versus the black rhino's pointed browsing lip. White rhinos are predominantly grazers and often more sociable in groups. A heavy head and low-slung feeding posture suit a grassy diet.

Two named living subspecies are usually distinguished: the **southern white rhinoceros** and the **northern white rhinoceros**. The northern form is functionally extinct in the wild, with only a tiny remnant managed under extraordinary reproductive-conservation efforts; those animals must **not** be conflated with Dubbo's southern white rhinos. Conservation status at the species and subspecies level differs, so do not attach a northern-rhino headline to every white rhino.

## Current conservation evidence
Poaching for illegal horn trade and habitat security remain substantial threats. IRF's **State of the Rhino 2025** estimated **15,752 wild white rhinoceroses in Africa at the end of 2024**, compared with **17,464 in 2023**. These are dated estimates (across ages, not reproductive adults), not a census of zoo animals. White rhinos are the most numerous surviving rhinoceros species, but their count can fall rapidly where protection collapses.

Source: [International Rhino Foundation 2025 report](https://rhinos.org/blog/news-room/sotr2025/). For detailed IUCN status/subspecies figures, verify the latest assessment rather than quoting a decades-old press release as current.

## Breeding history at Taronga Western Plains Zoo
Taronga's historical retrospective records that **five white rhinos arrived in 1980** and a **calf was born in 1981** — the first successful Australasian zoo breeding for the species according to Taronga. A further seven rhinos were brought from South Africa to Monarto and Dubbo collaboratively in **2003** for regional breeding/genetic management. These events show decades of managed care, **not current herd size**.

Current zoo itineraries describe a **white rhino viewing stop at 11F** and white rhinos in the African Savannah precinct. The [African Savannah page](https://www.taronga.org.au/dubbo-zoo/precincts-and-pitstops/african-savannah) also mentions giraffe, zebra and eland; mixed-species viewing areas do not prove particular individuals were present on a specific visit.

## Questions to investigate while visiting
- Is it mostly eating grass with a broad flat mouth, or selecting leaves with a hooked lip?
- Are there several adults within sight, and are young present? **Do not infer parentage.**
- How does a heavy grazing animal remain cool?
- Why might poaching statistics, wild global estimates and zoo breeding counts tell different stories?
